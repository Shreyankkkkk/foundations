# Fitting Trend Lines to Scatterplots

## Lesson 1 : Bivariate Relationship — Linearity, Strength, and Direction

### Bivariate Data

**Bivariate data** involves two variables and is often displayed using a **scatterplot**.

A scatterplot helps us look for a relationship between the two variables.

When describing a bivariate relationship, focus on:

1. **Direction** → positive or negative
2. **Strength** → strong or weak
3. **Form** → linear or non-linear
4. **Outliers** → unusual points far from the general pattern

---

### Direction

#### Positive Relationship

As one variable increases, the other variable tends to **increase**.

The overall trend slopes **upward** from left to right.

$$
\boxed{\text{Positive} \rightarrow \text{both variables tend to increase together}}
$$

#### Negative Relationship

As one variable increases, the other variable tends to **decrease**.

The overall trend slopes **downward** from left to right.

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative} \rightarrow \text{one increases while the other decreases}}
$$

---

### Linearity

A relationship is **linear** when the points can be reasonably described by a straight line.

The line does not have to pass through every point.

It should capture the **general trend** of the data.

$$
\boxed{\text{Linear} \rightarrow \text{roughly follows a straight-line pattern}}
$$

A relationship is **non-linear** when a straight line does not describe the pattern well.

The points may follow a **curve** instead.

$$
\boxed{\text{Non-linear} \rightarrow \text{pattern bends or follows a curve}}
$$

---

### Strength

Strength describes how closely the points follow the overall pattern.

- **Strong relationship** → points are close to the general trend/line.
- **Weak relationship** → points are more scattered around the trend.

For example:

$$
\boxed{\text{Strong} \rightarrow \text{points tightly clustered around the trend}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Weak} \rightarrow \text{points widely scattered around the trend}}
$$

Strength is somewhat **subjective** when judging a scatterplot by eye.

---

### Outliers

An **outlier** is a point that lies unusually far from the general pattern or cluster of points.

For example, if most points follow an upward trend but one point is far away from that trend, that point may be an outlier.

Outliers can affect how we describe the relationship, so they should be noticed when interpreting a scatterplot.

---

### Describing a Scatterplot

A useful description combines the main characteristics:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Direction + Strength + Form + Outliers}
}
$$

For example:

> **Strong, positive, linear relationship with no significant outliers.**

Or:

> **Weak, negative, linear relationship with an outlier.**

Or:

> **Strong, negative, non-linear relationship.**

---

### Examples of Patterns

#### Strong Positive Linear

- Points are close to an upward-sloping line.
- As one variable increases, the other tends to increase.

$$
\boxed{\text{Positive + Strong + Linear}}
$$

#### Weak Positive Linear

- Overall trend is upward.
- Points are widely scattered.

$$
\boxed{\text{Positive + Weak + Linear}}
$$

#### Strong Negative Linear

- Points are close to a downward-sloping line.
- As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative + Strong + Linear}}
$$

#### Negative Non-linear

- Variables move in opposite directions.
- The pattern follows a curve rather than a straight line.

$$
\boxed{\text{Negative + Strong + Non-linear}}
$$

---

### Important Distinction

A relationship can be **strong but non-linear**.

Strength does not determine whether the relationship is linear.

For example, points can closely follow a curved pattern:

$$
\boxed{\text{Strong + Non-linear}}
$$

Similarly, a relationship can be **weak but linear** if the overall trend is straight but the points are widely scattered.

---

### Key Takeaway

> **When describing a scatterplot, look at its direction, strength, form, and any outliers.**

Remember:

- **Positive** → variables tend to increase together.
- **Negative** → one tends to increase while the other decreases.
- **Linear** → roughly follows a straight line.
- **Non-linear** → follows a curve or another non-straight pattern.
- **Strong** → points are close to the overall pattern.
- **Weak** → points are more scattered.
- **Outlier** → point unusually far from the general pattern.

The core idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Scatterplot}
\rightarrow
\text{Direction + Strength + Form + Outliers}
}
$$

## Lesson 2 : Line of Best Fit

### What is a Line of Best Fit?

A **line of best fit** is a straight line that represents the **overall trend** of the data points in a scatterplot.

If a good line can describe the pattern of the points, there is evidence of a **linear relationship**.

$$
\boxed{\text{Good line of best fit} \rightarrow \text{linear trend}}
$$

If no reasonable line can describe the data, we say there is **no linear correlation**.

$$
\boxed{\text{No good line} \rightarrow \text{no linear trend}}
$$

---

### How to Identify a Good Line

A good line of best fit should:

- Follow the **overall trend** of the data.
- Represent the points reasonably well.
- Take **all of the data points** into consideration.

You should **not ignore points** simply because they do not fit the trend.

For example, if most points suggest an upward trend but several points are far below it, we cannot simply remove those points and draw a line through the remaining data.

---

### Important Idea

When deciding whether a line of best fit exists, ask:

> **Can I draw one straight line that reasonably represents the overall pattern without ignoring important data points?**

If yes:

$$
\boxed{\text{A good line of best fit exists}}
$$

If no:

$$
\boxed{\text{No good line of best fit exists}}
$$

---

### Linear Correlation vs No Linear Correlation

#### Linear correlation

The points generally follow a straight-line pattern.

$$
\boxed{\text{Points show a clear overall straight-line trend}}
$$

#### No linear correlation

The points do not have a reasonable straight-line pattern.

Even if a line can be drawn through part of the data, it is **not** a good line of best fit if doing so requires ignoring other important points.

---

### Key Takeaway

> **A line of best fit should represent the overall pattern of all the data points. Do not ignore points that do not fit the trend.**

Remember:

- Good straight-line fit → **linear trend**
- No reasonable straight-line fit → **no linear correlation**
- **Never ignore data points** just to make a line look better.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Good line of best fit}
\rightarrow
\text{linear relationship}
}
$$

## Lesson 3 : Using a Line of Best Fit to Estimate Values

### Estimating Values from a Scatterplot

A **line of best fit** can be used to estimate a value that is not directly shown in the data.

The general process is:

1. Identify the overall trend.
2. Draw or extend a line of best fit.
3. Find the desired value on the horizontal axis.
4. Move to the line and read the corresponding value on the vertical axis.

---

### Example: Smoking Rates

The scatterplot shows the percentage of American adults who smoked over time.

The horizontal axis represents:

$$
\text{years since 1965}
$$

Therefore:

- \(0\) → 1965
- \(-5\) → 1960
- \(-10\) → 1955
- \(-15\) → 1950
- \(-20\) → **1945**

The data shows a **negative linear relationship**:

$$
\boxed{\text{As time increases, the percentage of smokers decreases.}}
$$

---

### Extrapolation

To estimate the smoking rate in 1945, extend the line of best fit **backward** to \(20\) years before 1965.

This is an example of **extrapolation** because we are estimating a value outside the range of the observed data.

From the graph:

$$
\boxed{\text{Estimated smoking rate in 1945}\approx51\text{\%}-52\text{\%}}
$$

---

### Using the Rate of Change

The line decreases by roughly **10 percentage points every 20 years**.

So if the percentage was approximately \(41\%-42\%\) in 1965, going 20 years backward means increasing the percentage by about \(10\) percentage points:

$$
41\%-42\%+10\%\approx51\%-52\%
$$

This gives approximately the same estimate as extending the line visually.

---

### Important Distinction

**Interpolation** estimates a value **within** the range of observed data.

**Extrapolation** estimates a value **outside** the range of observed data by extending the observed trend.

Here, 1945 is outside the observed range, so we are using:

$$
\boxed{\text{Extrapolation}}
$$

Extrapolation depends on the assumption that the observed trend continues beyond the available data.

---

### Key Takeaway

> **A line of best fit can be extended to estimate values not directly shown in a scatterplot.**

For this example:

$$
1965-20=1945
$$

and extending the negative linear trend gives:

$$
\boxed{\text{1945 smoking rate}\approx51\text{\%}-52\text{\%}}
$$

Remember:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Scatterplot}
\rightarrow
\text{Line of best fit}
\rightarrow
\text{Extend trend}
\rightarrow
\text{Estimate value}
}
$$

- Within observed range → **interpolation**
- Outside observed range → **extrapolation**

# Analyzing Trend Lines in Scatterplots

## Lesson 1 : Interpreting Slope of Regression Line

### Interpreting the Slope

A **regression line** or **trend line** can be used to describe the relationship between two variables and make predictions.

The **slope** tells us how much the predicted value of the **dependent variable** changes when the **independent variable** increases by 1 unit.

For example, suppose a scatterplot shows the relationship between:

- **Independent variable (x)** → hours spent studying
- **Dependent variable (y)** → test score
- **Slope** → \(15\)

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{For every additional 1 hour of studying, the predicted test score increases by 15 points.}}
$$

The phrase **"predicted"** or **"on average"** is important because the regression line describes the overall trend, not what will happen to every individual student.

---

### Example

Suppose the regression line has:

$$
\text{slope}=15
$$

This means:

$$
\boxed{+1\text{ hour of studying}\rightarrow +15\text{ predicted test-score points}}
$$

For example:

- 1 additional hour → about 15 more points
- 2 additional hours → about 30 more points
- 3 additional hours → about 45 more points

The slope describes the change in the **vertical variable** for a 1-unit change in the **horizontal variable**.

---

### Independent vs. Dependent Variable

In this example:

$$
x=\text{hours studying}
$$

$$
y=\text{test score}
$$

Therefore:

- **Hours studying** → independent variable
- **Test score** → dependent variable

The slope should be interpreted as:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\text{change in predicted test score}}
{\text{change in hours studying}}
}
$$

So a slope of \(15\) means:

$$
\boxed{
15\text{ points per additional hour}
}
$$

It does **not** mean:

$$
15\text{ hours per additional point}
$$

The latter reverses the variables and incorrectly interprets the slope.

---

### How to Interpret a Positive Slope

A **positive slope** means that as the independent variable increases, the predicted dependent variable also increases.

In this example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{More study time}
\rightarrow
\text{higher predicted test score}
}
$$

The regression line slopes upward from left to right.

---

### Slope vs. Intercept

Do not confuse the **slope** with the **y-intercept**.

The **slope** tells us the predicted change in \(y\) for every 1-unit increase in \(x\).

The **y-intercept** tells us the predicted value of \(y\) when:

$$
x=0
$$

In the example, the model predicts that a student who studies \(0\) hours would score somewhere around \(35\)-\(40\) points.

That value comes from the **y-intercept**, not from the slope.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Slope}=15\rightarrow\text{change in predicted score per extra hour}}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{\text{Y-intercept}\rightarrow\text{predicted score when study time}=0}
$$

---

### Common Mistake

A slope of \(15\) does **not** mean:

> Students who do not study will score 15 points.

That would incorrectly treat the slope as the intercept.

It also does **not** mean:

> Study time increases by 15 hours for every additional point scored.

That reverses the roles of the variables.

The correct interpretation is:

> **The model predicts that the test score will increase by 15 points for each additional hour of study time.**

---

### General Rule for Interpreting Slope

If the regression equation is:

$$
\hat y=mx+b
$$

then:

- \(m\) = **slope**
- \(b\) = **y-intercept**
- \(\hat y\) = predicted value of the dependent variable

The slope \(m\) is interpreted as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{For every 1-unit increase in }x,
\text{ predicted }y\text{ changes by }m\text{ units.}
}
$$

If \(m>0\):

$$
\boxed{x\uparrow\rightarrow\hat y\uparrow}
$$

If \(m<0\):

$$
\boxed{x\uparrow\rightarrow\hat y\downarrow}
$$

---

### Key Takeaway

> **The slope tells you how much the predicted dependent variable changes for every 1-unit increase in the independent variable.**

For the study-time example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Slope}=15
\rightarrow
\text{15-point increase in predicted score per additional hour studied}
}
$$

Remember:

- **Slope** → rate of change
- **Y-intercept** → predicted \(y\) when \(x=0\)
- **Independent variable** → \(x\), usually on the horizontal axis
- **Dependent variable** → \(y\), usually on the vertical axis
- Interpret the slope using **units**.

$$\boxed{\text{Slope}=\dfrac{\text{change in predicted }y}{\text{change in }x}}$$

## Lesson 2 : Interpreting Y-Intercept in Regression Model

### What is the Y-Intercept?

The **y-intercept** of a regression line is the predicted value of \(y\) when the independent variable \(x\) equals \(0\).

For a regression equation:

$$
\hat y=mx+b
$$

- \(m\) = **slope**
- \(b\) = **y-intercept**
- \(\hat y\) = predicted value of \(y\)

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x=0\rightarrow\hat y=b}
$$

---

### Example: Coach Salary and Winning Percentage

A study looks at the relationship between:

- **Independent variable (\(x\))** → coach's yearly salary, measured in millions of dollars
- **Dependent variable (\(y\))** → team's winning percentage
- **Y-intercept** → \(39\)

Therefore, the model predicts:

$$
\boxed{
\text{When salary}=0\text{ million dollars},
\text{ predicted winning percentage}\approx39\%
}
$$

In words:

> **According to the model, teams whose coaches have a salary of \$0 million are predicted to have an average winning percentage of approximately 39%.**

---

### Y-Intercept vs. Slope

It is important not to confuse the y-intercept with the slope.

#### Y-Intercept

The y-intercept tells us the predicted value of \(y\) when:

$$
x=0
$$

In this example:

$$
\boxed{0\text{ million dollars}\rightarrow39\text{\%}\text{ predicted winning percentage}}
$$

#### Slope

The slope tells us how much the predicted \(y\) changes for every 1-unit increase in \(x\).

For example, if the slope were \(5\):

$$
\boxed{
+1\text{ million dollars in salary}
\rightarrow
+5\text{ percentage points in predicted winning percentage}
}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Y-intercept}\rightarrow\text{starting/predicted value at }x=0}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Slope}\rightarrow\text{rate of change}}
$$

---

### A Useful Question to Ask

Whenever you need to interpret a y-intercept, ask:

> **What does \(x=0\) represent in this situation?**

Then determine the predicted \(y\)-value at that point.

For this example:

1. \(x\) = coach salary
2. \(x=0\) means the coach earns \$0 million
3. The y-intercept is \(39\)
4. Therefore, predicted winning percentage = \(39\%\)

---

### Be Careful With Real-World Meaning

A regression model can give a mathematical prediction that is **unrealistic or outside the meaningful range of the data**.

In this example, a coach earning exactly \$0 may not be realistic.

Therefore, although the mathematical interpretation is:

$$
\boxed{0\text{ salary}\rightarrow39\text{\%}\text{ predicted winning percentage}}
$$

we should be cautious about treating that prediction as a realistic real-world conclusion.

This is especially important when \(x=0\) is **outside the range of the observed data**.

---

### Common Mistakes

#### Mistake 1: Confusing the y-intercept with the average

The y-intercept of \(39\) does **not** mean:

> The average winning percentage was 39%.

It means:

> The model predicts a winning percentage of approximately 39% when the coach's salary is 0.

---

#### Mistake 2: Confusing the y-intercept with the slope

The y-intercept of \(39\) does **not** mean:

> Every \$1 million increase in salary is associated with a 39 percentage-point increase in winning percentage.

That type of statement describes the **slope**, not the y-intercept.

---

#### Mistake 3: Assuming the prediction is automatically realistic

The regression model predicts \(39\%\) at \(x=0\), but that does not guarantee that the prediction is realistic.

Always consider whether:

$$
\boxed{x=0\text{ makes sense in the real-world context}}
$$

---

### General Rule

If a regression equation is:

$$
\hat y=mx+b
$$

then the y-intercept is \(b\).

Its interpretation is:

> **When the independent variable is 0 units, the model predicts the dependent variable will be \(b\) units.**

In symbols:

$$
\boxed{
x=0\Rightarrow\hat y=b
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaway

> **The y-intercept tells you the predicted value of the dependent variable when the independent variable is 0.**

For the coach salary example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Y-intercept}=39
}
$$

means:

$$\boxed{\text{At a salary of \$0 million, the model predicts approximately a 39}\text{\%}\text{ winning percentage.}}$$

Remember:

- **Slope** → how much \(y\) changes when \(x\) increases by 1
- **Y-intercept** → predicted \(y\) when \(x=0\)
- Always check whether \(x=0\) is **realistic and within the data's range**.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Y-intercept}
\rightarrow
x=0
\rightarrow
\text{predicted }y
}
$$

## Lesson 3 : Correlation Coefficient Intuition

### What is the Correlation Coefficient?

The **correlation coefficient**, usually written as \(r\), measures how well a **linear model** describes the relationship between two variables.

It also tells us the **direction** of the linear relationship:

- \(r>0\) → positive correlation
- \(r<0\) → negative correlation
- \(r\approx0\) → little or no linear correlation

The value of \(r\) is always between \(-1\) and \(1\):

$$
\boxed{-1\le r\le1}
$$

---

### Understanding \(r=1\)

An \(r\) value of \(1\) represents a **perfect positive linear relationship**.

As one variable increases, the other variable also increases, and the points lie perfectly along a straight line.

$$
\boxed{r=1\rightarrow\text{perfect positive linear correlation}}
$$

For example:

- \(x\) increases → \(y\) increases.
- The points can be described perfectly by an upward-sloping line.

---

### Understanding \(r=-1\)

An \(r\) value of \(-1\) represents a **perfect negative linear relationship**.

As one variable increases, the other variable decreases, and the points lie perfectly along a straight line.

$$
\boxed{r=-1\rightarrow\text{perfect negative linear correlation}}
$$

For example:

- \(x\) increases → \(y\) decreases.
- The points can be described perfectly by a downward-sloping line.

---

### Understanding \(r=0\)

An \(r\) value close to \(0\) means that there is **little or no linear relationship** between the variables.

The points do not follow a clear straight-line pattern.

$$
\boxed{r\approx0\rightarrow\text{little or no linear correlation}}
$$

Different lines could potentially be drawn through the data without one clearly representing the relationship.

---

### Strength and the Correlation Coefficient

The **absolute value** of \(r\) describes the strength of the linear relationship.

The closer \(r\) is to \(1\) or \(-1\), the stronger the linear correlation.

The closer \(r\) is to \(0\), the weaker the linear correlation.

$$
\boxed{|r|\text{ close to }1\rightarrow\text{strong linear relationship}}
$$

$$
\boxed{|r|\text{ close to }0\rightarrow\text{weak linear relationship}}
$$

For example:

$$
r=-0.72
$$

indicates a fairly strong **negative** linear relationship.

While:

$$
r=0.65
$$

indicates a moderately strong **positive** linear relationship.

And:

$$
r=-0.02
$$

indicates almost **no linear correlation**.

---

### Sign vs. Magnitude of \(r\)

It is important to separate two ideas:

#### Sign → Direction

- Positive \(r\) → positive relationship
- Negative \(r\) → negative relationship

#### Magnitude → Strength

- \(|r|\) close to \(1\) → stronger linear relationship
- \(|r|\) close to \(0\) → weaker linear relationship

For example:

$$
r=-0.72
$$

means:

- **Negative** → variables tend to move in opposite directions.
- **0.72 in magnitude** → the linear relationship is fairly strong.

---

### Matching \(r\) to a Scatterplot

When matching correlation coefficients to scatterplots, first look for the **direction** of the relationship.

Then consider how closely the points follow a straight line.

A useful process is:

1. Determine whether the trend is **positive, negative, or neither**.
2. Estimate how well a **straight line** fits the points.
3. Choose the sign of \(r\) based on the direction.
4. Choose the magnitude of \(r\) based on the strength of the linear pattern.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Direction}\rightarrow\text{sign of }r
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Linear strength}\rightarrow\text{magnitude of }r
}
$$

---

### Example: Scatterplot A

Suppose the scatterplot shows that:

- When \(x\) is small, \(y\) tends to be large.
- When \(x\) is large, \(y\) tends to be small.
- The points follow a fairly clear downward linear trend.

This indicates a **negative** and relatively strong correlation.

If the choices include:

$$
0.65,\quad0.84,\quad-0.02,\quad-0.72
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{r=-0.72}
$$

is a reasonable match.

The negative sign comes from the downward trend, while the relatively large magnitude indicates a fairly strong relationship.

---

### Example: Scatterplot B

Suppose the points show a clear **positive** linear trend.

As \(x\) increases, \(y\) tends to increase.

If the choices are:

$$
r=0.65
\quad\text{and}\quad
r=0.84
$$

we compare how closely the points follow a line.

If the points in scatterplot B are closer to the trend line than those in another positive scatterplot, B should receive the **larger correlation coefficient**:

$$
\boxed{r=0.84}
$$

---

### Example: Scatterplot C

Suppose the points are scattered in many directions with no obvious linear pattern.

There is no clear indication that \(y\) increases or decreases as \(x\) increases.

Therefore, the correlation should be close to zero.

$$
\boxed{r=-0.02}
$$

The value is slightly negative, but its magnitude is extremely small:

$$
|-0.02|=0.02
$$

so it represents essentially **no linear correlation**.

---

### Example: Scatterplot D

Suppose scatterplot D also has a positive trend, but its points are more scattered around the line than those in scatterplot B.

Then D has a positive correlation, but it is weaker than B.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{r=0.65}
$$

while the stronger positive relationship in B receives:

$$
\boxed{r=0.84}
$$

---

### Comparing Correlation Coefficients

Consider:

$$
r=0.84
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
r=0.65
$$

Both indicate positive relationships, but:

$$
|0.84|>|0.65|
$$

Therefore, \(r=0.84\) represents the **stronger linear relationship**.

Similarly:

$$
r=-0.72
$$

is stronger than:

$$
r=-0.02
$$

because:

$$
|-0.72|>|-0.02|
$$

The negative sign tells us the direction; it does **not** mean that the relationship is weaker.

---

### Important Idea

The correlation coefficient measures how well a **linear model** describes the relationship.

It does **not** simply measure whether two variables have any relationship at all.

A scatterplot could have a clear curved pattern while having a correlation coefficient near zero because a straight line does not describe the relationship well.

$$
\boxed{
r\text{ measures linear association}
}
$$

---

### Real-World Data

Perfect values of \(r=1\) or \(r=-1\) are uncommon in real-world data.

Real datasets usually have points that do not lie exactly on a straight line.

Therefore, when interpreting a real scatterplot, we generally ask:

> **How strong is the linear trend, and in which direction does it go?**

The closer the points are to a straight-line pattern, the closer \(r\) will generally be to \(1\) or \(-1\).

---

### Key Takeaway

> **The correlation coefficient \(r\) describes the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two variables.**

Remember:

- \(r=1\) → **perfect positive linear correlation**
- \(r=-1\) → **perfect negative linear correlation**
- \(r\approx0\) → **little or no linear correlation**
- Positive \(r\) → **positive direction**
- Negative \(r\) → **negative direction**
- \(|r|\) close to \(1\) → **stronger linear relationship**
- \(|r|\) close to \(0\) → **weaker linear relationship**

The core idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation coefficient}
\rightarrow
\text{Direction + Strength of linear relationship}
}
$$

When matching \(r\) to a scatterplot:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sign of }r\rightarrow\text{direction}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
|r|\rightarrow\text{strength of linear fit}
}
$$

## Lesson 4 : Correlation Does Not Imply Causality

### Correlation vs. Causality

A major idea in statistics is that **correlation does not imply causality**.

Two variables can be related without one variable causing the other.

#### Causality

**Causality** means that one variable directly causes a change in another variable.

$$
\boxed{A\text{ causes }B}
$$

For example, a true causal relationship would mean that changing \(A\) produces a change in \(B\).

#### Correlation

**Correlation** means that two variables tend to occur or change together.

$$
\boxed{A\text{ and }B\text{ tend to occur together}}
$$

Correlation alone does **not** tell us which variable causes the other, or whether either variable causes the other.

---

### The Important Distinction

Suppose we observe:

$$
\boxed{\text{Breakfast eating}\leftrightarrow\text{lower obesity}}
$$

A correlation tells us that these variables are associated.

It does **not** automatically tell us:

$$
\boxed{\text{Eating breakfast}\rightarrow\text{lower obesity}}
$$

The second statement is a **causal claim**, and additional evidence is needed to support it.

---

### Example: Breakfast and Obesity

A study observed teenagers over several years and found that students who regularly ate breakfast tended to:

- gain less weight,
- have a lower body mass index,
- be more physically active.

These findings show that the variables are **correlated**.

However, the study by itself does not necessarily establish that:

$$
\boxed{\text{Eating breakfast causes lower obesity}}
$$

or that:

$$
\boxed{\text{Skipping breakfast causes obesity}}
$$

The observed relationship could have other explanations.

---

### Why Correlation Alone Is Not Enough

When two variables are correlated, there are several possible explanations.

#### Possibility 1: \(A\) causes \(B\)

For example:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

Perhaps eating breakfast affects some factor that reduces obesity.

But this is only one possible explanation.

---

#### Possibility 2: \(B\) causes \(A\)

The direction of causality could be reversed:

$$
B\rightarrow A
$$

For example, another factor related to a person's weight or health could influence whether they feel hungry in the morning and therefore whether they eat breakfast.

The correlation alone cannot determine the direction of causality.

---

#### Possibility 3: A third variable causes both

A third variable \(C\) could influence both \(A\) and \(B\):

$$
C\rightarrow A
$$

and

$$
C\rightarrow B
$$

This is especially important when interpreting observational studies.

---

### Example of a Third Variable

Suppose **physical activity** is related to both breakfast and obesity.

It could be that:

$$
\text{Physical activity}
\rightarrow
\text{more likely to eat breakfast}
$$

and

$$
\text{Physical activity}
\rightarrow
\text{lower obesity}
$$

Then breakfast and obesity would be correlated even if breakfast itself were not causing the difference in obesity.

The relationship could look like:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Physical activity}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Breakfast eating}\\
\text{Lower obesity}
\end{cases}
}
$$

In this situation, physical activity is a **confounding variable** or possible underlying factor.

---

### Another Possible Third Variable

**Sleep** could also potentially be related to both variables.

For example:

$$
\text{Sleep habits}
\rightarrow
\text{Breakfast habits}
$$

and

$$
\text{Sleep habits}
\rightarrow
\text{Weight/health outcomes}
$$

If this were the underlying explanation, simply changing breakfast habits might not produce the expected effect.

The important point is not that sleep is definitely the cause, but that **other explanations must be considered**.

---

### Correlation Does Not Tell Us the Cause

Suppose we observe:

$$
A\leftrightarrow B
$$

From correlation alone, we cannot conclude that:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

We also cannot automatically conclude:

$$
B\rightarrow A
$$

And there may be a third variable:

$$
C\rightarrow A
$$

and

$$
C\rightarrow B
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation alone does not establish cause and effect.}
}
$$

---

### Observational Studies

An **observational study** observes variables as they naturally occur.

Researchers may find that two variables are strongly associated, but this does not automatically prove that changing one variable will cause the other to change.

For example, if researchers observe that breakfast eaters tend to have lower obesity rates, the study can establish an association if properly conducted.

But we should be cautious about turning that observation into:

> Eating breakfast prevents obesity.

That is a much stronger **causal claim**.

---

### Why Headlines Can Be Misleading

A news article might report a correlation using language that suggests causation.

For example:

> "Eating breakfast may beat teen obesity."

A reader might interpret this as:

$$
\text{Eat breakfast}\rightarrow\text{avoid obesity}
$$

But the underlying research may only have established:

$$
\text{Breakfast eating}\leftrightarrow\text{lower obesity}
$$

These statements are **not equivalent**.

The first makes a causal claim.

The second describes an association.

---

### Be Careful With Causal Language

When reading research or news articles, watch for words such as:

- causes
- prevents
- leads to
- results in
- makes
- reduces
- increases

These words often imply **causality**.

A study showing correlation may justify more cautious language such as:

- associated with
- correlated with
- related to
- tends to occur with

---

### What Correlation Can Tell Us

If two variables are correlated, we can say that they tend to vary together.

For example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A and B are associated}
}
$$

But correlation does not tell us:

1. Which variable causes which.
2. Whether either variable causes the other.
3. Whether a third variable causes both.

---

### What Would Provide Stronger Evidence for Causality?

A study designed to investigate **cause and effect** is stronger evidence for causality than simply observing naturally occurring relationships.

In particular, **well-designed experiments** can help researchers investigate whether changing one variable produces a change in another.

The key distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}\rightarrow\text{association}
}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Causality}\rightarrow\text{cause and effect}
}
$$

A correlation can provide evidence worth investigating, but it does not by itself prove causation.

---

### Important Example

Suppose a study finds:

$$
\text{Breakfast eating}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Lower obesity}
$$

Possible explanations include:

$$
\text{Breakfast}\rightarrow\text{Lower obesity}
$$

or:

$$
\text{Lower obesity}\rightarrow\text{Breakfast eating}
$$

or:

$$
\text{Physical activity}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Breakfast eating}\\
\text{Lower obesity}
\end{cases}
$$

or another underlying factor could explain the relationship.

Therefore, we should not immediately choose one explanation simply because two variables are correlated.

---

### Important Distinction

A correlation can be **real** without being **causal**.

For example, suppose the data clearly shows:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A and B are correlated}
}
$$

That finding may be completely valid.

The mistake is to automatically conclude:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A causes B}
}
$$

without sufficient evidence.

---

### Key Takeaway

> **Correlation does not imply causality.**

Remember:

- **Correlation** → two variables are associated or tend to occur together.
- **Causality** → one variable causes a change in another.
- A correlation does not tell us which variable causes the other.
- A third variable may cause both observed variables.
- Observational data can show association without proving cause and effect.
- Be especially careful when news headlines turn correlations into causal claims.

The core idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\neq
\text{Causation}
}
$$

When you see a relationship between two variables, ask:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Are they correlated, or do we have evidence that one causes the other?}
}
$$
