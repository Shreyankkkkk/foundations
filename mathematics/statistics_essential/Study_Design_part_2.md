# Potential Problems with Sampling

## Lesson 1 : Bias in surveys

## Lesson 1 : Examples of Bias in Surveys

### Sources of Bias

**Bias** occurs when the method used to collect data causes the results to systematically differ from the true population values.

Common sources of bias include:

- **Voluntary response**
- **Convenience sampling**
- **Nonresponse**
- **Response bias**
- **Undercoverage**

---

### Voluntary Response Bias

A **voluntary response sample** occurs when people choose themselves to participate.

People who feel strongly about the topic may be more likely to respond.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Voluntary response}
=
\text{people choose whether to participate}
}
$$

**Example:** A podcast host asks listeners to visit his website and vote on how much they like his show.

Fans are more likely to participate, so the results may **overestimate** how much the entire audience likes the show.

> **Memory:** People volunteer → people with strong opinions are more likely to respond.

---

### Convenience Sampling

A **convenience sample** uses people who are easy for the researcher to reach.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Convenience sample}
=
\text{choose people because they are easy to access}
}
$$

**Example:** The podcast host surveys the next 100 listeners who sent him fan emails.

These people are especially likely to like the host, so the sample may not represent all listeners.

> **Memory:** Convenient ≠ representative.

---

### Nonresponse Bias

**Nonresponse** occurs when selected individuals do not respond to the survey.

If the people who do not respond differ meaningfully from those who do, the results can be biased.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Nonresponse}
=
\text{selected people do not participate}
}
$$

**Example:** 100 people are asked to complete a survey, but only 97 respond.

The 3 people who did not respond could have different opinions from the 97 who did.

> **Memory:** Selected but don't answer → nonresponse.

---

### Response Bias

**Response bias** occurs when people give inaccurate or untruthful answers.

This can happen when:

- a question is sensitive
- people do not want to admit something
- the wording of the question influences the answer

Examples:

- "Have you lied to your parents?"
- "Have you ever cheated on your spouse?"
- "Do you smoke?"

People may not answer these questions honestly.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Response bias}
=
\text{answers are systematically inaccurate}
}
$$

> **Memory:** The problem is with the **answer**, not who was selected.

---

### Undercoverage

**Undercoverage** occurs when an important part of the population is left out of the sampling process.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Undercoverage}
=
\text{some population groups are not adequately represented}
}
$$

For example, if a survey method cannot reach a particular group of people, that group may be underrepresented.

> **Memory:** The sampling method **misses part of the population**.

---

### Comparing the Main Biases

| Bias                   | Main problem                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Voluntary response** | People choose themselves to participate          |
| **Convenience**        | People are chosen because they are easy to reach |
| **Nonresponse**        | Selected people do not respond                   |
| **Response bias**      | People give inaccurate or influenced answers     |
| **Undercoverage**      | Part of the population is missed                 |

---

### Example: David's Podcast

David wants to know how much his listeners like his show.

#### Situation 1

He asks listeners to visit his website and participate in a poll.

The biggest concern is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Voluntary response bias}}
$$

Listeners who already strongly like David may be more likely to participate.

Therefore, the reported **89%** who love the show may be an **overestimate**.

---

#### Situation 2

David polls the next 100 listeners who sent him fan emails.

The biggest concern is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Convenience sampling}}
$$

These listeners are easy to access, but they are likely to be unusually positive toward David.

Even though some people do not respond, **convenience sampling is the larger concern**.

---

### Quick Exam Strategy

When identifying bias, ask:

**1. Did people choose themselves to participate?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Voluntary response}}
$$

**2. Were people chosen because they were easy to reach?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Convenience}}
$$

**3. Were selected people asked but some did not respond?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Nonresponse}}
$$

**4. Could people be giving inaccurate answers?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Response bias}}
$$

**5. Is an important part of the population missing from the sampling method?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Undercoverage}}
$$

---

### Key Takeaways

> **Voluntary response:** people choose to participate.

> **Convenience:** people are chosen because they are easy to reach.

> **Nonresponse:** selected people do not answer.

> **Response bias:** people give inaccurate or influenced answers.

> **Undercoverage:** part of the population is missed.

A useful distinction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Who gets selected?}
\rightarrow
\text{Sampling bias}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Who actually responds?}
\rightarrow
\text{Nonresponse / voluntary response}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{What answers are given?}
\rightarrow
\text{Response bias}
}
$$

### Memory Trick

> **Volunteer = choose themselves**  
> **Convenience = easy to reach**  
> **Nonresponse = don't answer**  
> **Response bias = bad/inaccurate answers**  
> **Undercoverage = people left out**

## Lesson 2 : Example of Undercoverage Introducing Bias

### Undercoverage

**Undercoverage** occurs when part of the population cannot be selected by the sampling method.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Undercoverage}
=
\text{part of the population is left out of the sampling frame}
}
$$

This can cause bias if the excluded group has different opinions or characteristics from the people who can be sampled.

---

### Example: Internet Privacy Poll

A senator wants to know how concerned people in her state are about internet privacy.

She:

- randomly selects **100 names from a phone book**
- calls those people
- keeps calling until all 100 respond
- finds that **42%** are very concerned about internet privacy

However:

- mobile phone numbers are not in the phone book
- unlisted numbers are not in the phone book

Therefore, some people in the population **cannot be selected**.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Undercoverage}
}
$$

is the main source of bias.

---

### Why Is It Not Nonresponse?

The senator's office keeps calling until **all 100 selected people respond**.

Therefore, there are no selected people who failed to respond.

$$
\boxed{
\text{No nonresponse problem}
}
$$

Remember:

> **Nonresponse = selected people do not answer.**

Here, everyone selected eventually answered.

---

### Why Is It Not Voluntary Response?

The senator did not ask people to volunteer for the survey.

Instead, she **randomly selected people from the phone book** and contacted them.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Not voluntary response}
}
$$

Remember:

> **Voluntary response = people choose themselves to participate.**

---

### Why Is There Undercoverage?

The target population is **all people in the state**, but the sampling frame is only people listed in the phone book.

People with:

- mobile phones only
- unlisted numbers

cannot be selected.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Target population}
\neq
\text{population covered by sampling frame}
}
$$

---

### Direction of the Bias

People who choose to have unlisted numbers or only use mobile phones may be more concerned about privacy.

Therefore, the survey's **42%** is likely an **underestimate** of the true percentage of people in the state who are very concerned about internet privacy.

$$
\boxed{
42\%
<
\text{true percentage}
}
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Undercoverage}
\rightarrow
\text{likely underestimate}
}
$$

in this example.

---

### Quick Exam Strategy

When you see a sampling problem, ask:

**1. Did selected people fail to respond?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Nonresponse}}
$$

**2. Did people choose themselves to participate?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Voluntary response}}
$$

**3. Is part of the population missing from the sampling frame?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Undercoverage}}
$$

For this example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Phone book}
\rightarrow
\text{misses mobile/unlisted numbers}
\rightarrow
\text{Undercoverage}
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaway

> **Undercoverage happens when the sampling method cannot reach part of the population.**

In the senator's survey:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Phone book}
\rightarrow
\text{misses some people}
\rightarrow
\text{Undercoverage}
\rightarrow
\text{42\% likely underestimates true concern}
}
$$

### Memory Trick

> **Undercoverage = "Who's missing?"**

If an important group **cannot be selected in the first place**, think **undercoverage**.

# Sampling Methods

## Lesson 1 : Techniques for Generating a Simple Random Sample

### Simple Random Sample

A **simple random sample (SRS)** is a sample where individuals are selected randomly so that each member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

For example, from **80 students**, we want to randomly select **30** to estimate the population's average height.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}=80
\qquad
\text{Sample}=30
}
$$

The goal is to make the selection genuinely random.

---

### Method 1: Drawing Names

A simple way to create an SRS:

1. Give every student a unique name/number.
2. Put all 80 names on identical pieces of paper.
3. Mix them thoroughly.
4. Randomly draw **30 names without replacement**.

$$
\boxed{
\text{80 names}
\rightarrow
\text{randomly draw 30}
\rightarrow
\text{SRS}
}
$$

**Without replacement** means once someone is selected, they cannot be selected again.

---

### Method 2: Random Number Generator

Assign each student a unique number.

For 80 students:

$$
01,02,03,\ldots,79,80
$$

Then use a random number generator to select numbers from **1 through 80**.

Keep the first **30 unique valid numbers**.

Ignore:

- numbers outside the range
- repeated numbers

Example:

$$
59,\ 83,\ 59,\ 37,\ 00,\ 91,\ 23,\ldots
$$

Valid selections so far:

$$
\boxed{59,\ 37,\ 23}
$$

- **83** → ignore
- **59** → already selected
- **00** → ignore
- **91** → ignore

Continue until 30 different students are selected.

---

### Method 3: Random Digit Table

A **random digit table** provides a sequence of randomly generated digits.

If students are numbered **01–80**, read the table in two-digit groups.

For example:

$$
59,\ 83,\ 59,\ 37,\ 00,\ 91,\ 23,\ldots
$$

Use the same rules:

$$
\boxed{
01\text{–}80=\text{use}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
00,\ 81\text{–}99=\text{ignore}
}
$$

Repeated valid numbers are also ignored.

Continue until the required sample size is reached.

---

### Why Not Pick Numbers Yourself?

Humans are not good at producing truly random choices.

For example, someone might unintentionally:

- favor certain numbers
- avoid repeats
- create patterns
- think some numbers "look random"

Therefore, use an actual random process such as:

- drawing names
- a random number generator
- a random digit table

$$
\boxed{
\text{Actual random process}
>
\text{human judgment of randomness}
}
$$

---

### Quick Exam Strategy

If asked how to generate a **simple random sample**:

**Step 1:** Give every population member a unique identifier.

**Step 2:** Use a genuinely random process.

**Step 3:** Select the required number of unique individuals.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Number everyone}
\rightarrow
\text{Randomly select}
\rightarrow
\text{Sample}
}
$$

Remember:

> **No choosing based on personal judgment.**

---

### Key Takeaways

A simple random sample can be generated by:

$$
\boxed{\text{Drawing names randomly}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Random number generator}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Random digit table}}
$$

When selecting without replacement:

$$
\boxed{
\text{No individual can be selected twice}
}
$$

The main idea is:

> **Every member of the population should have an equal chance of being selected.**

### Memory Trick

> **Number everyone → Randomize → Take the sample.**

## Lesson 2 : Techniques for Random Sampling and Avoiding Bias

### Random Sampling

Random samples reduce the chance of bias and better represent the population.

### Simple Random Sample

Randomly select individuals from the entire population.

> **Memory:** Everyone has a chance → randomly pick individuals.

### Stratified Sample

Divide the population into important groups (**strata**) and randomly sample from each group.

Example: 25 freshmen, 25 sophomores, 25 juniors, 25 seniors.

> **Memory:** Split into groups → sample from **each** group.

### Clustered Sample

Divide the population into groups (**clusters**), randomly select clusters, then survey **everyone** in the selected clusters.

> **Memory:** Randomly pick **groups** → survey everyone in them.

### Non-Random Sampling

- **Voluntary:** People choose to participate → likely biased.
- **Convenience:** Choose whoever is easiest to reach → may not represent the population.

### Other Sources of Bias

**Question wording:** Leading or loaded wording can influence answers.

**Response bias:** People give inaccurate answers or don't answer honestly because of fear, sensitivity, or pressure.

### Quick Comparison

| Method            | What happens                                    |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Simple random** | Randomly select individuals                     |
| **Stratified**    | Sample from every important group               |
| **Cluster**       | Randomly select groups, survey everyone in them |
| **Voluntary**     | People choose themselves                        |
| **Convenience**   | Choose whoever is easiest                       |

### Memory Trick

> **Simple = individuals**  
> **Stratified = some from every group**  
> **Cluster = whole groups**  
> **Voluntary = choose themselves**  
> **Convenience = easiest to reach**

## Lesson 3 : Systematic Random Sampling

### Systematic Random Sample

A **systematic random sample** selects members at a fixed interval after choosing a **random starting point**.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random start}
\rightarrow
\text{select every }k\text{th person}
}
$$

**Example:**  
10,000 concert attendees → want about 100 people.

- Randomly choose the **37th** person as the starting point.
- Then survey every **100th** person: 37th, 137th, 237th, etc.
- The **100-person interval** is called the **sample interval**.

### Why Use It?

It is often easier than creating a complete list of everyone in the population.

It also spreads the sample throughout the population instead of taking only the first people encountered.

> **Memory:** Random start + fixed interval.

### Important: It Can Still Be Biased

Systematic sampling is **not automatically unbiased**.

If there is a pattern in how people are arranged, the fixed interval could repeatedly select a particular type of person.

**Example:** If groups of people enter in a repeating pattern—such as 50 people from the parking lot followed by 50 people from taxis—sampling every 100th person could consistently favor one group.

> **Memory:** Watch for **patterns matching the sampling interval**.

### Quick Comparison

| Method                | How people are selected                         |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Simple random**     | Randomly select individuals                     |
| **Systematic random** | Random start, then every \(k\)th person         |
| **Stratified**        | Randomly sample from each group                 |
| **Cluster**           | Randomly select groups, survey everyone in them |

### Key Takeaway

$$
\boxed{
\text{Systematic sampling}
=
\text{random starting point + fixed interval}
}
$$

It is efficient, but always check whether the population has a pattern that could introduce bias.

# Experimental Designs

## Lesson 1 : Introduction to Experiment Design

### Explanatory & Response Variables

- **Explanatory variable:** The variable that may cause a change.
- **Response variable:** The outcome being measured.

**Example:** Testing a diabetes pill:

- Pill/treatment → **explanatory variable**
- Change in A1C → **response variable**

> **Memory:** Explanatory = explains the change; Response = responds to it.

### Random Sampling vs. Random Assignment

A good experiment uses both when possible:

1. **Random sampling:** Randomly choose people from the population → helps make results **generalizable**.
2. **Random assignment:** Randomly place participants into treatment/control groups → helps establish **causation**.

> **Memory:** Sampling = **who gets studied**. Assignment = **who gets which treatment**.

### Control & Treatment Groups

- **Treatment group:** Receives the treatment being tested.
- **Control group:** Does not receive the actual treatment and provides a comparison.

### Block Design

If a characteristic could affect the response, divide participants into **blocks** based on that characteristic, then randomly assign within each block.

Example: If sex may affect the drug's effectiveness, separately randomize males and females so both treatment groups have similar proportions.

> **Memory:** Block = **group similar people first, then randomize within groups**.

### Placebo

A **placebo** looks like the real treatment but contains no active treatment.

It controls for the **placebo effect**—people may improve simply because they believe they are receiving treatment.

### Blind & Double-Blind

- **Blind:** Participants don't know whether they receive the treatment or placebo.
- **Double-blind:** Neither participants **nor the people administering the experiment** know who receives which treatment.

This reduces psychological influence and bias.

### Drawing Conclusions

If the treatment group improves significantly more than the control group, and the difference is unlikely to be due to chance, the experiment provides evidence of a **causal relationship**.

Replication with different populations and settings strengthens confidence in the result.

### Key Takeaway

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random assignment + control group + placebo/blinding}
\rightarrow
\text{stronger evidence of causation}
}
$$

## Lesson 2 : Experiment Design Considerations

### Why Random Assignment Matters

Randomly assigning participants to **control** and **treatment** groups helps balance other factors between the groups.

However, random assignment does **not guarantee** perfectly balanced groups.

For example, by chance, the treatment group might contain more young people, while the control group contains more older people. If age affects the response, **age becomes a lurking variable** that could distort the results.

> **Memory:** Random assignment reduces lurking-variable imbalance, but cannot eliminate it completely.

### Block Design

A **block design** groups participants according to an important characteristic before randomly assigning them.

Example:

- 60 women → randomly split between treatment/control
- 40 men → randomly split between treatment/control

This ensures both groups have similar proportions of men and women.

> **Memory:** Block = separate by an important characteristic, then randomize within each block.

### Matched Pairs Design

A **matched pairs design** goes further by having participants experience **both conditions**.

Example:

1. Group A receives the treatment for 3 months.
2. Group B receives the placebo for 3 months.
3. The groups switch.
4. Compare each person's response under treatment vs. placebo.

Because the **same people experience both conditions**, characteristics such as age, location, and individual behavior are naturally controlled for.

> **Memory:** Matched pairs = **same person, both treatments**.

### Why Matched Pairs Helps

With separate treatment/control groups, random chance can create differences between groups.

With matched pairs, each person acts as their **own comparison**, greatly reducing the effect of individual differences.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Same person in both conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{less lurking-variable imbalance}
}
$$

### Blinding Still Matters

Matched-pairs experiments can still use **blinding** so participants don't know whether they're receiving the treatment or placebo.

Double-blinding can also prevent researchers administering the treatment from influencing participants.

### No Experiment Is Perfect

Even a carefully designed experiment can have limitations and lurking variables.

Therefore, **replication** is important: repeating the experiment with different populations, locations, or conditions helps determine whether the result is reliable.

### Quick Comparison

| Design                | Main idea                                              |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Random assignment** | Randomly place people into groups                      |
| **Block design**      | Group by an important characteristic, then randomize   |
| **Matched pairs**     | Each participant receives both conditions              |
| **Double-blind**      | Participants and administrators don't know assignments |

### Key Takeaway

The goal of experimental design is to make treatment and control conditions as comparable as possible so that differences in the response can be attributed to the **explanatory variable**, rather than lurking variables.

## Lesson 3 : Correlation Does Not Imply Causality

### Correlation vs. Causality

One of the most important ideas in statistics is that **correlation does not imply causation**.

**Correlation** means two variables tend to occur or change together.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
=
\text{A and B tend to occur together}
}
$$

**Causality** means a change in one variable directly produces a change in another.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Causality}
=
\text{A causes B}
}
$$

A correlation between two variables does **not** tell us which variable causes the other—or whether a third variable causes both.

_>_ **\*\*Memory:\*\*** _Together ≠ because of._

**---**

### Example: Breakfast and Obesity

An article reports that teenagers who regularly eat breakfast:

- tend to be more physically active
- tend to gain less weight
- tend to have lower BMI
- are less likely to be obese

The article may make it sound like:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Eating breakfast}
\rightarrow
\text{less obesity}
}
$$

However, the study described is primarily showing a **correlation**.

The data show that:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Breakfast eating}
\leftrightarrow
\text{lower obesity}
}
$$

But this does **not** prove:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Breakfast eating}
\rightarrow
\text{lower obesity}
}
$$

The difference is extremely important.

**---**

### What Correlation Tells Us

If two variables are correlated, we know they tend to occur together.

For example:

$$
\text{Eating breakfast}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Lower obesity}
$$

This means teenagers who eat breakfast tend to have lower obesity rates.

But correlation alone cannot tell us **why**.

There are several possible explanations.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{Causation}
}
$$

**---**

### Possibility 1: A Causes B

Maybe eating breakfast really does help prevent obesity.

$$
\text{Breakfast}
\rightarrow
\text{Lower obesity}
$$

If this is the true explanation, then breakfast could have a causal effect.

However, the correlation by itself does not prove this.

**---**

### Possibility 2: B Causes A

The direction of causality could be reversed.

For example, perhaps teenagers with lower body fat are more likely to feel hungry in the morning.

Then:

$$
\text{Lower body fat}
\rightarrow
\text{More likely to eat breakfast}
$$

In this situation, eating breakfast is **not necessarily causing** lower obesity.

Instead, the relationship could be going in the opposite direction.

_>_ **\*\*Memory:\*\*** _Correlation doesn't tell you which way the arrow points._

**---**

### Possibility 3: A Third Variable Causes Both

A third variable may influence both variables.

This is one of the most important reasons correlation does not imply causality.

For example, suppose **physical activity** is the underlying variable.

$$
\text{Physical activity}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{More likely to eat breakfast}\\
\text{Less likely to be obese}
\end{cases}
$$

Then we observe:

$$
\text{Breakfast}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Lower obesity}
$$

even though breakfast itself may have little or no causal effect on obesity.

The physical activity is creating the observed association.

_>_ **\*\*Memory:\*\*** _A hidden third variable can create a correlation._

**---**

### Possible Third Variable: Sleep

Another possible explanation could be **sleep**.

Suppose insufficient sleep causes:

- people to wake up late and skip breakfast
- changes in behavior that increase the likelihood of obesity

Then:

$$
\text{Lack of sleep}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Skipping breakfast}\\
\text{Higher obesity}
\end{cases}
$$

Again, breakfast and obesity would be correlated even if breakfast itself were not the cause.

**---**

### Possible Third Variable: Poverty

Economic conditions could also affect both variables.

For example, poverty might make someone:

- more likely to skip breakfast
- more likely to have limited access to healthy food
- more likely to experience other conditions associated with obesity

This could create:

$$
\text{Poverty}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Skipping breakfast}\\
\text{Higher obesity}
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore, simply telling someone to eat breakfast may not address the underlying cause.

**---**

### The Three Main Possibilities

When two variables are correlated, consider:

**1. A causes B**

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

**2. B causes A**

$$
B\rightarrow A
$$

**3. A third variable causes both**

$$
C\rightarrow A
$$

$$
C\rightarrow B
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation alone cannot establish causality}
}
$$

**---**

### Correlation vs. Causality

| Concept                       | Meaning                                            |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **\*\*Correlation\*\***       | Two variables tend to occur together               |
| **\*\*Causality\*\***         | One variable produces a change in another          |
| **\*\*Reverse causation\*\*** | B may cause A instead of A causing B               |
| **\*\*Third variable\*\***    | Another variable causes or influences both A and B |

**---**

### Why This Matters

A common mistake is to see:

$$
A\leftrightarrow B
$$

and immediately conclude:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

That conclusion is not justified by correlation alone.

For example:

> Teenagers who eat breakfast tend to be less obese.

This is a **correlational statement**.

It does **not** automatically mean:

> Eating breakfast prevents obesity.

That second statement is a **causal claim**.

_>_ **\*\*Memory:\*\*** _Observed together does not mean one caused the other._

**---**

### A Better Way to Think About Studies

When reading a study, ask:

**1. What variables are being compared?**

For example:

$$
\text{Breakfast habits}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{Obesity}
$$

**2. Does the study show correlation or causality?**

If researchers simply observe people and record their behaviors, be careful about making causal conclusions.

**3. Could the direction be reversed?**

Maybe:

$$
B\rightarrow A
$$

instead of:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

**4. Could a third variable explain the relationship?**

Ask:

$$
C\rightarrow A
\quad\text{and}\quad
C\rightarrow B
$$

**5. What kind of study was performed?**

A well-designed randomized experiment can provide much stronger evidence for causality than an observational study.

**---**

### Observational Study vs. Experiment

An **observational study** observes variables without assigning treatments.

It can reveal strong correlations, but hidden variables and other explanations may remain.

A **randomized experiment** randomly assigns participants to treatments.

Random assignment helps balance lurking variables between groups, making it much easier to determine whether the treatment caused a change in the response.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random assignment}
\rightarrow
\text{stronger evidence of causation}
}
$$

This connects directly to the earlier lesson on **experimental design**.

**---**

### Important Distinction

Do **not** say:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation means no relationship}
}
$$

That is incorrect.

Correlation tells us that there **is an association**.

The problem is that correlation alone does not tell us the **cause** of that association.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\rightarrow
\text{association}
}
$$

but

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\not\rightarrow
\text{causation}
}
$$

**---**

### Quick Exam Strategy

When a problem gives you a correlation, ask:

**\*\*1. Does A cause B?\*\***

Maybe—but correlation alone cannot prove it.

**\*\*2. Could B cause A?\*\***

Consider reverse causation.

**\*\*3. Could another variable cause both?\*\***

Look for a lurking or confounding variable.

**\*\*4. Was there random assignment?\*\***

If there was a properly designed randomized experiment, causal conclusions are much more justified.

**---**

### Key Takeaways

_>_ **\*\*Correlation:\*\*** _two variables tend to occur together._

_>_ **\*\*Causality:\*\*** _one variable causes a change in another._

_>_ **\*\*Correlation does not imply causality.\*\***

_>_ _The direction of causality could be reversed._

_>_ _A third variable could cause both variables._

_>_ _Observational studies can show correlation but generally cannot establish causation by themselves._

_>_ _Randomized experiments provide stronger evidence for causality._

The central idea is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A and B are correlated}
\neq
\text{A causes B}
}
$$

**---**

### Memory Trick

_>_ **\*\*Correlation = together\*\***

_>_ **\*\*Causation = because of\*\***

_>_ **\*\*Correlation? Ask "Why?"\*\***

When you see:

$$
A\leftrightarrow B
$$

ask:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A causes B?}
\quad
\text{B causes A?}
\quad
\text{Or C causes both?}
}
$$

# Inference and Exeperiments

## Lesson 1 : Statistical Significance

### Statistical Significance

**Statistical significance** helps us determine whether an observed difference is likely to have happened **by chance**.

In an experiment, we might observe a difference between two groups. The important question is:

> **Could this difference have happened just because of random variation?**

If the answer is "very unlikely," we have evidence that the treatment may have had a real effect.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Statistical significance}
=
\text{results are unlikely to be explained by chance alone}
}
$$

**---**

### Example: Food Advertisements and Eating

Researchers randomly assigned **500 children** to two groups:

- **Treatment group:** watched food commercials
- **Control group:** watched non-food commercials

After watching the cartoon, researchers measured how many grams of crackers each child ate.

The children who watched food commercials ate, on average, **10 grams more** than the children who watched non-food commercials.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Difference in means}=10\text{ grams}
}
$$

At first, this looks like evidence that food advertisements caused children to eat more.

But there is an important question:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Could a 10-gram difference happen by chance?}
}
$$

**---**

### Simulating What Would Happen by Chance

To answer this, researchers used a **simulation**.

They took the same 500 children's results and repeatedly **randomly reassigned** them into two new groups.

The important idea is that the original treatment labels are removed.

They then calculated:

$$
\text{Mean of Group 1}
-
\text{Mean of Group 2}
$$

for each simulation.

They repeated this **150 times**.

This creates a distribution showing the differences we might expect **if there were no real treatment effect**.

_>_ **\*\*Memory:\*\*** _Randomly reshuffle the results → see how often a difference like the observed one occurs by chance._

**---**

### Comparing the Actual Result to the Simulation

The actual experiment produced a difference of:

$$
10\text{ grams}
$$

In the 150 simulations, a difference this large occurred only about **1 time**.

Therefore:

$$
P(\text{difference of this size by chance})
\approx
\frac{1}{150}
\approx 0.67\%
$$

This is less than **1%**.

That means a difference of 10 grams would be very unusual if the treatment had no real effect.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Very unlikely by chance}
\rightarrow
\text{statistically significant}
}
$$

**---**

### The 5% Rule

A commonly used significance level is **5%**.

If the probability of obtaining results this extreme by chance is less than 5%, the result is often considered **statistically significant**.

$$
\boxed{
p<0.05
\rightarrow
\text{statistically significant}
}
$$

In this experiment:

$$
p\approx0.007
$$

which is less than 0.05.

Therefore, the result is statistically significant.

**---**

### What Does This Tell Us?

Because the children were **randomly assigned** to the two groups, the experiment provides evidence for a **causal effect**.

The observed difference is unlikely to be explained by random chance alone.

Therefore, the experiment provides evidence that:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Food commercials may cause children to eat more crackers}
}
$$

However, statistical significance does **not** mean that the result is guaranteed to be true or that the effect is necessarily large or important.

It means the observed result would be unusual if there were no real effect.

**---**

### Quick Exam Strategy

When you see a simulation question:

**1. Find the observed difference.**

$$
\boxed{10\text{ grams}}
$$

**2. Look at how often simulations produce a difference at least this extreme.**

**3. Calculate the approximate probability.**

$$
\frac{\text{number of extreme simulations}}
{\text{total simulations}}
$$

**4. Compare it with 5%.**

$$
\boxed{
p<0.05
\rightarrow
\text{statistically significant}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
p\geq0.05
\rightarrow
\text{not statistically significant}
}
$$

**---**

### Key Takeaways

_>_ **\*\*Statistical significance:\*\*** _the observed result is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone._

_>_ _A simulation can show how often a result this extreme occurs by chance._

_>_ _The smaller the probability, the stronger the evidence against chance being the explanation._

_>_ **\*\*5% is a common cutoff.\*\***

_>_ _Statistical significance does not mean the result is absolutely certain._

The main idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observed result}
\rightarrow
\text{simulate chance}
\rightarrow
\text{compare}
\rightarrow
\text{determine significance}
}
$$

### Memory Trick

_>_ **\*\*Rare by chance = significant\*\***

If the result would happen only rarely when the treatment has no effect, we have evidence that something real may be happening.

## Lesson 2 : Confounding Variables and Causal Relationships

### Can We Establish Causality?

To conclude that one variable **causes** another, we need a well-designed experiment.

For example:

$$
\text{Dietary program}
\rightarrow
\text{Weight loss}
$$

The important question is whether the dietary program actually caused the additional weight loss.

**---**

### Example: Gym Dietary Program

A gym offers an optional dietary program for an extra fee.

The manager:

- randomly samples **50 members who joined the program**
- randomly samples **50 members who did not**
- compares their weight loss over the past 3 months

The program participants lost significantly more weight.

The manager concludes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The dietary program caused the increased weight loss}
}
$$

This conclusion is **not valid**.

**---**

### The Problem: Self-Selection

The key issue is that members **chose whether to participate**.

They were not randomly assigned to the dietary program.

$$
\boxed{
\text{People chose their own groups}
}
$$

This means the two groups could differ in important ways besides the dietary program.

For example, people who choose the program might:

- be more motivated to lose weight
- have more money to spend on the program
- already have healthier habits

These differences could affect weight loss.

**---**

### Confounding Variable

A **confounding variable** is a variable that is related to both the explanatory variable and the response variable, making it difficult to determine whether the explanatory variable actually caused the observed difference.

For example:

$$
\text{Motivation}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{Join dietary program}\\
\text{Lose more weight}
\end{cases}
$$

The program and weight loss are associated, but motivation could be responsible for some or all of the difference.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Confounding variable}
\rightarrow
\text{cannot isolate the causal effect}
}
$$

**---**

### What Can the Manager Conclude?

The manager **can** conclude that there is an **association** between participating in the dietary program and greater weight loss.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Dietary program}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Greater weight loss}
}
$$

But the manager **cannot conclude** that the dietary program caused the weight loss.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Association}
\neq
\text{Causation}
}
$$

**---**

### How Could We Establish Causality?

A better experiment would:

1. Take a random sample of members.
2. **Randomly assign** them to treatment and control groups.
3. Require the treatment group to participate in the dietary program.
4. Compare their weight loss.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Random assignment}
\rightarrow
\text{Compare outcomes}
}
$$

Random assignment helps balance other characteristics between the groups, reducing the effect of confounding variables.

**---**

### Important Distinction

Randomly selecting people is **not the same** as randomly assigning them.

**Random sampling:**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Who gets included in the study?}
}
$$

**Random assignment:**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Who gets which treatment?}
}
$$

For establishing **causality**, random assignment is especially important.

In this gym example, the manager randomly sampled from each group, but the members had already **self-selected** into the groups.

**---**

### Quick Exam Strategy

When a study claims that one variable caused another, ask:

**1. Were people randomly assigned to treatments?**

- **Yes** → causal conclusions may be possible.
- **No** → look for confounding variables.

**2. Did people choose their own treatment?**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Self-selection}
\rightarrow
\text{possible confounding}
}
$$

**3. What conclusion is safe?**

If there is no random assignment, you can usually say:

$$
\boxed{
\text{There is an association}
}
$$

but not:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A caused B}
}
$$

### Key Takeaways

*>* **\*\*Random sampling:\*\*** *helps determine who is studied and supports generalizing results.*

*>* **\*\*Random assignment:\*\*** *helps establish causality.*

*>* **\*\*Self-selection:\*\*** *can create confounding variables.*

*>* **\*\*Confounding variable:\*\*** *an outside variable that can explain the observed relationship.*

*>* *Without random assignment, an association does not automatically establish causation.*

### Memory Trick

*>* **\*\*Sampling = who?\*\***

*>* **\*\*Assignment = treatment?\*\***

*>* **\*\*Self-select = beware confounding.\*\***

The main idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Self-selected groups}
\rightarrow
\text{possible confounding}
\rightarrow
\text{association, not causation}
}
$$
