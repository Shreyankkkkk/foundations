# Intoduction to Plannning a Study

## Lesson 1 : Identifying a Sample and Population

### Population

A **population** is the **entire group of individuals** that a study is interested in.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{entire group being studied}
}
$$

The population is the group that researchers ultimately want to learn about.

---

### Sample

A **sample** is a **subset of the population** that is actually observed, surveyed, or measured.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{part of the population actually studied}
}
$$

The sample is used to gather information about the larger population.

---

### Example: Riverview High School

Administrators at Riverview High School want to know how seniors feel about the lunch offerings in the school's cafeteria.

They randomly survey **100 seniors**.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all seniors at Riverview High School}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{the 100 seniors who were surveyed}
}
$$

The 100 seniors are only a subset of all the seniors at the school.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}\subseteq\text{Population}
}
$$

---

### Population vs Sample

| Term           | Meaning                  | Riverview Example             |
| -------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| **Population** | Entire group of interest | All seniors at Riverview High |
| **Sample**     | Subset actually studied  | 100 seniors surveyed          |

A useful way to remember the difference:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{WHO we want information about}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{WHO we actually collect information from}
}
$$

---

### Why Use a Sample?

Researchers often cannot or do not want to collect data from every member of the population.

Studying the entire population may be:

- impractical
- expensive
- time-consuming
- difficult to organize

Instead, researchers can select a sample and use its results to learn about the population.

The basic process is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
\rightarrow
\text{Sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Collect data}
\rightarrow
\text{Learn about population}
}
$$

---

### Random Sample

A **random sample** is a sample selected randomly from the population.

In the Riverview example, the administrators randomly select 100 seniors.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random sample}
=
\text{sample selected randomly from the population}
}
$$

Random sampling helps reduce the risk that the sample is systematically different from the population.

---

### Identifying the Population

When identifying the population, ask:

> **Who are the researchers trying to learn about?**

For the Riverview example, the researchers want to know how **all seniors at Riverview High** feel about the cafeteria.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all seniors at Riverview High}
}
$$

It is **not**:

- all high school seniors in the world
- all students at Riverview High
- all teenagers

The population must match the group the study is actually trying to understand.

---

### Identifying the Sample

When identifying the sample, ask:

> **Who was actually surveyed, observed, or measured?**

In the Riverview example, the administrators surveyed 100 seniors.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{the 100 seniors surveyed}
}
$$

It is not all seniors at the school because only 100 of them were actually surveyed.

---

### Common Mistakes

#### Mistake 1: Making the population too large

Suppose the study is about seniors at one particular school.

Incorrect:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all high school seniors in the world}
}
$$

Correct:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all seniors at the school}
}
$$

The population depends on the **specific group the researchers want to learn about**.

---

#### Mistake 2: Calling the entire population the sample

If 100 seniors were surveyed, the sample is not all seniors.

Incorrect:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{all seniors at Riverview}
}
$$

Correct:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{100 seniors who were surveyed}
}
$$

---

### Example

A university wants to know how students feel about its online classes.

The university randomly surveys 500 students.

Then:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all students at the university}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{the 500 students surveyed}
}
$$

The 500 students provide information that can be used to learn about the larger population.

---

### Quick Exam Strategy

When a question asks you to identify the population and sample:

**Step 1:** Ask who the researchers want to learn about.

$$
\boxed{\text{Population}}
$$

**Step 2:** Ask who was actually surveyed, observed, or measured.

$$
\boxed{\text{Sample}}
$$

Remember:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
\rightarrow
\text{larger group}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
\rightarrow
\text{smaller group actually studied}
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaways

> **Population = the entire group we want to learn about.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{entire group of interest}
}
$$

> **Sample = the part of the population that we actually study.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{subset actually studied}
}
$$

The most important relationship is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}\subseteq\text{Population}
}
$$

For the Riverview example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all seniors at Riverview High}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{100 seniors surveyed}
}
$$

### Memory Trick

> **Population = WHO you WANT to know about.**
>
> **Sample = WHO you ACTUALLY ask or measure.**

The overall idea:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Use data to learn about}
\rightarrow
\text{Population}
}
$$

## Lesson 2 : Generalizing Results to a Population

### What Does It Mean to Generalize?

**Generalizing** means using the results from a sample to make conclusions about a **larger population**.

For example, researchers may survey a sample of students and use their responses to learn about a larger group of students.

The basic idea is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample results}
\rightarrow
\text{Conclusions about a population}
}
$$

However, we can only safely generalize to a population that the sample **properly represents**.

---

### Example: University Residence Halls

A large university has **24 residence halls**.

Estella oversees **12 of those residence halls**.

Her department takes a **large simple random sample** of first-year students who live in those 12 residence halls.

The students are surveyed about their overall satisfaction with campus living.

The important details are:

- The sample contains **first-year students**.
- They live in **Estella's 12 residence halls**.
- The sample was **large**.
- The sample was selected using a **simple random sample**.

Therefore, the survey results can safely be generalized to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{All first-year students who live in those 12 residence halls}
}
$$

---

### Why Can't We Generalize to All First-Year Students?

The sample only includes first-year students who live in the **12 residence halls managed by Estella**.

There may be other first-year students living in the university's other 12 residence halls.

Those students were not part of the population from which the sample was selected.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Cannot generalize to all first-year students at the university}
}
$$

The sample may not represent students living in the other residence halls.

For example, Estella's 12 residence halls could be:

- the closest to campus
- the furthest from campus
- unusually good
- unusually poor
- different in some other way

Because the sample only comes from those 12 halls, we cannot assume that the results apply to students living elsewhere.

---

### Why Does Simple Random Sampling Matter?

A **simple random sample** gives members of the population a fair chance of being selected.

If the sample is a large simple random sample from a particular population, the results can generally be generalized to that population.

The key relationship is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Can generalize to the population sampled from}
}
$$

But remember:

> **Random sampling does not automatically allow you to generalize to everyone.**

You can only generalize to the population from which the random sample was actually selected.

---

### The Sampling Population

The **sampling population** is the group from which the sample was actually selected.

In this example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sampling population}
=
\text{first-year students living in the 12 residence halls}
}
$$

The sample is randomly selected from this group.

Therefore, this is the population to which the results can be generalized.

---

### Important Distinction

There are three groups to keep track of:

1. **The entire university**
2. **The 12 residence halls managed by Estella**
3. **The students actually surveyed**

The survey only samples from group 2.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{first-year students living in the 12 residence halls}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{large random sample of those students}
}
$$

---

### What About Students Beyond Their First Year?

The survey only sampled **first-year students**.

Second-year, third-year, and fourth-year students were not included.

Therefore, the results cannot safely be generalized to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{All students living in the 12 residence halls}
}
$$

Why?

Because students beyond their first year may have different experiences and opinions about campus living.

The sample was specifically drawn from **first-year students**.

---

### Generalization Rule

When deciding whether survey results can be generalized, ask:

> **Who could have been selected for the sample?**

The answer identifies the population to which the results can be generalized.

For example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample selected from population}
\rightarrow
\text{Results can be generalized to that population}
}
$$

But:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample selected from population A}
\not\rightarrow
\text{Automatically generalizable to population B}
}
$$

---

### Example

Suppose a school has 1,000 students.

Researchers randomly survey 100 students from the entire school.

Then the results can potentially be generalized to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{All 1,000 students at the school}
}
$$

But suppose instead they randomly survey 100 students from only the school's basketball team.

Then the results can only be generalized to the population represented by that sampling process, such as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Students on the basketball team}
}
$$

You cannot use the basketball team's opinions to automatically represent the entire school.

---

### Large Sample vs Population Coverage

A **large sample** is useful, but size alone does not determine what population you can generalize to.

For example, suppose you survey 5,000 students from only one residence hall.

Even though:

$$
\boxed{\text{Sample size}=5000}
$$

you still cannot automatically generalize the results to students living in every residence hall.

The sample must represent the population you want to make conclusions about.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Large sample}
\neq
\text{automatically representative of everyone}
}
$$

The **sampling method and sampling population** matter.

---

### Key Question for Exams

When asked:

> **"To which population can these results be generalized?"**

Ask:

### Step 1: Who was actually sampled?

Identify the group from which the researchers selected participants.

### Step 2: Was the sample randomly selected?

If it was a random sample, the results can generally be generalized to the population from which that sample was drawn.

### Step 3: Check for restrictions

Look for restrictions such as:

- only first-year students
- only students in certain residence halls
- only members of a particular group
- only people from a particular location

The generalization must respect these restrictions.

---

### Riverview Connection

This connects directly to Lesson 1.

In Lesson 1:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Population}
=
\text{all seniors at Riverview High}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
=
\text{100 seniors surveyed}
}
$$

Because the 100 seniors were randomly sampled from the seniors at the school, the survey results can potentially be generalized to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{all seniors at Riverview High}
}
$$

The same principle applies here.

---

### Important Exam Rule

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random sample}
\rightarrow
\text{generalize to the population sampled from}
}
$$

But:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Do not generalize beyond the population represented by the sample}
}
$$

For the university example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Generalize to:}
\text{first-year students in the 12 residence halls}
}
$$

Not:

$$
\boxed{
\text{All students at the university}
}
$$

and not:

$$
\boxed{
\text{All first-year students at the university}
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaways

> **A survey's results can only be generalized to the population from which the sample was appropriately selected.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Sampling population}
\rightarrow
\text{Generalization}
}
$$

A **large simple random sample** allows us to make conclusions about the population from which the sample was drawn.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Large simple random sample}
\rightarrow
\text{Generalizable to its sampling population}
}
$$

But remember:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random sampling does not mean you can generalize to everyone}
}
$$

You must respect the characteristics of the sampled population.

### Memory Trick

> **Ask: "Who could have been picked?"**

That group is the population you can generalize to.

For the university example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Who could have been picked?}
=
\text{first-year students living in the 12 residence halls}
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Generalize to that group}
}
$$

## Lesson 3 : Types of Statistical Studies

### Experiment vs Observational Study

The two main types of statistical studies are:

### Experiment

An **experiment** actively assigns subjects to different groups and applies a treatment or condition.

Typically:

- Subjects are assigned to a **treatment group** or **control group**.
- The treatment group receives the treatment.
- The control group may receive a placebo or no treatment.
- The results are compared between the groups.

$$
\boxed{\text{Experiment}\rightarrow\text{actively assign treatments and observe results}}
$$

### Observational Study

An **observational study** does **not** assign treatments or actively put subjects into groups.

Instead, researchers collect and analyze data about what already exists.

$$
\boxed{\text{Observational study}\rightarrow\text{observe and collect data without assigning treatment}}
$$

---

### Types of Observational Studies

There are three important types:

| Type              | What it does                           | Time direction |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| **Retrospective** | Uses past data                         | Looks backward |
| **Prospective**   | Collects data going forward            | Looks forward  |
| **Sample survey** | Collects information from a sample now | Present        |

---

#### Retrospective Study

A **retrospective study** looks at data from the **past**.

Researchers use existing records or previously collected information.

Memory trick:

> **Retro = backward**

$$
\boxed{\text{Retrospective}\rightarrow\text{past data}}
$$

**Example:** Analyzing students' Khan Academy usage from previous years.

---

#### Prospective Study

A **prospective study** follows a sample **into the future** and collects data over time.

Researchers select the sample first, then track the subjects.

Memory trick:

> **Prospective = forward**

$$
\boxed{\text{Prospective}\rightarrow\text{future data}}
$$

**Example:** Select 100 people and track their daily habits for the next year.

---

#### Sample Survey

A **sample survey** collects information from a sample **at the present time**, usually by asking questions or recording current information.

$$
\boxed{\text{Sample survey}\rightarrow\text{collect information now}}
$$

**Example:** Randomly selecting students and asking them how satisfied they currently are with their school.

---

### Quick Decision Guide

Ask these questions:

**1. Are researchers actively assigning a treatment?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Experiment}}
$$

**2. No treatment is assigned. Are they using past data?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Retrospective}}
$$

**3. No treatment is assigned. Are they tracking a sample into the future?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Prospective}}
$$

**4. No treatment is assigned. Are they collecting information from people now?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Sample survey}}
$$

---

#### Key Takeaway

$$
\boxed{
\text{Experiment}
=
\text{assign treatment}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Retrospective}
=
\text{look backward}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Prospective}
=
\text{look forward}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Sample survey}
=
\text{ask/observe now}
}
$$

#### Memory Trick

> **Experiment = DO something**  
> **Retrospective = PAST**  
> **Prospective = FUTURE**  
> **Sample survey = NOW**

## Lesson 4 : Identifying Observational Studies

### Observational Study

An **observational study** collects data by observing existing conditions without assigning treatments.

The goal is often to identify a **relationship or correlation between variables**.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observational study}
=
\text{observe variables without assigning treatment}
}
$$

---

### Example: Naming Cows

Researchers surveyed farmers in Great Britain about:

- whether their cows were given names
- the cows' milk yield

They found that farms where cows were named had an average milk yield **258 liters higher** than farms where cows were not named.

This is an **observational study** because the researchers did not tell farmers whether to name their cows. They simply observed what was already happening.

---

### Why It Is Not an Experiment

In an experiment, researchers would actively assign farmers to groups.

For example:

- Group 1 → name their cows
- Group 2 → do not name their cows

Then researchers would compare milk yields.

That did **not** happen in this study.

$$
\boxed{
\text{No treatment assignment}
\rightarrow
\text{Not an experiment}
}
$$

---

### Correlation vs Causation

The study found a **positive correlation** between naming cows and milk yield.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Naming cows}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Higher milk yield}
}
$$

However, this does **not** prove that naming cows causes higher milk production.

The correct conclusion is only that the variables are **associated/correlated** in the observed population.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\neq
\text{Causation}
}
$$

---

### Confounding Variable

A **confounding variable** is another variable that may be related to both variables being studied and could explain their association.

For example, a farmer's treatment of cows could affect both:

- whether cows are named
- milk production

Therefore, the observed relationship between naming cows and milk yield might be partly or entirely explained by another variable.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Confounding variable}
\rightarrow
\text{may influence both variables}
}
$$

---

### What Can Be Concluded?

Because this is an observational study, researchers can conclude that:

$$
\boxed{
\text{There is a positive correlation between naming cows and milk yield among the farmers studied}
}
$$

They **cannot** conclude:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Naming cows causes higher milk yield}
}
$$

---

### Quick Decision Guide

When identifying a study, ask:

**1. Did researchers actively assign a treatment?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Experiment}}
$$

**2. Did they simply observe existing conditions and look for relationships?**

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}\rightarrow\text{Observational study}}
$$

Then ask:

> **Can the study prove causation?**

For an observational study:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Usually no causal conclusion}
}
$$

Instead:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observational study}
\rightarrow
\text{association/correlation}
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaways

> **Observational study = observe without assigning treatment.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observational}
\rightarrow
\text{correlation/association}
}
$$

> **Correlation does not prove causation.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Correlation}
\neq
\text{Causation}
}
$$

> **Confounding variables can help explain an observed association.**

### Memory Trick

> **Experiment = DO something.**  
> **Observational = WATCH what happens.**  
> **Correlation = relationship, not proof of cause.**

## Lesson 5 : Understanding Causality

### Association vs Causality

An **association** means two variables are related.

**Causality** means a change in one variable actually **causes** a change in another.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Association}
\neq
\text{Causation}
}
$$

An observational study can show an association, but by itself it generally cannot prove that one variable causes another.

---

### Example: Smartphone Usage and Happiness

A study used approximately **5,000 randomly selected teenagers** and found that teenagers who spent more time on smartphones were, on average, less happy.

This shows an **association** between:

- smartphone usage
- happiness

$$
\boxed{
\text{More smartphone use}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Lower happiness}
}
$$

However, it does **not** prove:

$$
\boxed{
\text{More smartphone use}
\rightarrow
\text{Lower happiness}
}
$$

Jerry's conclusion that smartphone usage **causes** teenagers to be less happy is therefore not valid.

---

### Why Can't We Prove Causation?

There are several possible explanations for the association.

#### 1. Reverse Causation

The direction of causality could be reversed.

For example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Lower happiness}
\rightarrow
\text{More smartphone use}
}
$$

Less happy teenagers might use their smartphones more.

---

#### 2. Confounding Variable

A third variable could affect both variables.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Confounding variable}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{smartphone usage}\\
\text{happiness}
\end{cases}
}
$$

For example, another factor could make a teenager both less happy and more likely to use a smartphone.

---

### When Can We Make a Causal Conclusion?

A well-designed **experiment** can provide evidence for causality.

Researchers can:

1. Randomly assign subjects to groups.
2. Give the treatment group a treatment.
3. Give the control group no treatment or a placebo.
4. Compare the outcomes.

For example:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Random assignment}
\rightarrow
\text{Treatment vs Control}
\rightarrow
\text{Compare outcomes}
}
$$

Because the researchers actively assign the treatment, an experiment can establish evidence of a **cause-and-effect relationship**.

---

### Observational Study vs Experiment

| Study type | What researchers do | Causal conclusion? |
|---|---|---|
| **Observational study** | Observe existing conditions | Generally **no** |
| **Experiment** | Assign treatments to groups | **Can** support causality |

The key difference is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Experiment}
=
\text{researchers assign treatment}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observational study}
=
\text{researchers observe without assigning treatment}
}
$$

---

### Important Exam Rule

If a question describes a study where researchers **only observe** people's existing behavior:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Association/correlation}
\text{ can be concluded}
}
$$

but:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Causation}
\text{ cannot generally be concluded}
}
$$

If researchers **randomly assign a treatment**:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Experiment}
\rightarrow
\text{causal conclusions may be possible}
}
$$

---

### Key Takeaways

> **Observational study → association, not causation.**

> **Experiment with appropriate random assignment → can provide evidence of causation.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observe}
\rightarrow
\text{Association}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Randomly assign treatment}
\rightarrow
\text{Causality can be investigated}
}
$$

### Memory Trick

> **Observe = Association**  
> **Assign = Causation**
